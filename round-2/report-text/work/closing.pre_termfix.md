# What we have learned so far

After two iterations the run has a firm behavioural picture on two path search tasks, a causal answer to where a prohibition acts, and still no answer to what decides the replacement. Qwen3-8B writes BFS and Dijkstra almost without exception, and it is just as collapsed on 13 further coding tasks (modal share 1.00 on 12, 0.98 on one). Sampling temperature, top-k, top-p, thinking mode and random seeds change how the code is worded and not which algorithm it implements; with seeds coupled across temperatures the algorithm changed in at most 1 of 30 seeds per cell. A single prompt ban does not open the repertoire. When the model obeys it, it collapses again on one substitute: Bellman-Ford/SPFA in all five iteration-1 draws (272 of 272 compliant programs) and in all three iteration-2 draws, and DFS in four of five iteration-1 draws (116 of 128) and all three iteration-2 draws. With two or more names banned the model re-implements the banned mechanisms, often under another description, and with four or five names banned it writes the same mix of BFS and DFS whatever the list contains. How often a single ban is obeyed depends on its wording far more than on anything else measured: with five fixed templates the banned share was stable across draws (0.14 to 0.15 on the grid task, 0.11 to 0.16 on the shortest path task), and on the grid task one template ("X is not allowed here") produced every failure. Under one statistic, no ban and no diversity knob buys a second correct algorithm for less than 20 points of pass rate on either task. Only generate then select, which hands the choice to an external random draw [{{Wong2024}}], reaches three or four algorithms, at 24 to 42 points.

The substitute is predictable before the ban, from the model's free-form answer, its output when every algorithm is listed, and the base model's distribution, but no measurement so far says why. On the shortest path task every perturbation that moves the model away from Dijkstra, random activation directions included, lands on the same runner-up as the ban, so on that task a ban behaves like an arbitrary push. On the grid task only bans reach DFS. Measured by forward-pass likelihoods in two models, the survivors' gains under a ban are not pure subtraction, but their X-specific part is not aimed at the algorithm the ban's own name primes or at the model's stated alternative, and the generic perturbations do not form a coherent ladder either; the X-specific share is undetermined in Qwen3-8B (0.55, interval [0.25, 0.67]) and unresolved in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (0.80, [0.28, 0.94]). The model's ability to state its replacement in advance may be self-simulation [{{Binder2024}}] rather than evidence about how the ban is implemented. Naming an algorithm inside a prohibition raises that algorithm's likelihood when the model does not normally write it, in both models.

Where the ban acts is now measured. Cache patching shows that a ban is applied by the prompt states before the first generated token: in Qwen3-8B the prompt route carries 0.85 of the banned algorithm's likelihood drop and the direct route none, and Llama agrees (0.84). The visible mechanism is a veto on the default program's first line: under the grid ban no program opens with the import that 99% of neutral-prompt programs begin with. On the shortest path task behaviour follows the prompt route completely; on the grid task generated tokens also read the ban directly. The read position carries part of how strongly a wording will be obeyed (correlation -0.41 across 110 wordings) and nothing about what replaces X. The read-position geometry that iteration 1 built is low-dimensional and decodes the shortest path default, but the ban shift there is orthogonal to the algorithm directions, every activation edit at that position did nothing or broke the model, and geodesic proximity predicts the wrong substitute on the shortest path task. Decodable is not the same as actionable here, as elsewhere [{{Basu2026}}]. No decision locus has been found in any saved state: the evidence from the forking token screen, the locus probe and the opening-line veto all points to the first three generated tokens, which have not been saved. Mid-depth layers (12 to 23) are where blocking the ban segment restores the most on the grid task, in line with negation being constructed in middle layers rather than applied as a late switch [{{Zhou2026}}].

Post-training sharpened an ordering the base model shows under the chat template (preregistered verdicts: INSTALLED_BY_POST_TRAINING on the shortest path task, MIXED on the grid task, MIXED on both at the planned sample size), consistent with reports that post-training narrows output diversity [{{Kirk2023}}, {{West2025}}]; in plain completion the base model's shortest path mode is Bellman-Ford/SPFA. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is not collapsed on the grid task and splits over several algorithms on bench tasks where Qwen3-8B writes one, so the collapse is a property of the model as well as the task.

What remains open follows from this. The substitution claim needs the frozen 12-task bench, scored with unscrubbed programs and task-level clusters in both models, with Llama supplying the present-minor bans that Qwen3-8B's collapse removes. The algorithm decision needs states and patches at generated tokens 1 to 3, and the grid task's direct component needs its own localisation. Ban wording must be modelled as a random effect in every analysis. The request items not yet delivered are activation-space manifold plots at a validated locus, a sparse-autoencoder readout that passes its transfer test, and a thinking-on same-seed arm.
